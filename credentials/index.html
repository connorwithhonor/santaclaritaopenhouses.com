Santa Clarita Valley, a straight answer from a listing agent

The letters after an agent's name, decoded.

Every designation, certification and badge you are likely to see on a business card in this valley. What it takes to get it. What it really tells you. And the ten questions that outrank all of it.

I do not have a single one of them

Start here, because it is the reason this page exists. I hold none of these credentials. Not one.

I could have bought most of them in a weekend. A course, a fee, yearly dues, and the letters go on the card. That is the real requirement behind the majority of what you are about to read.

So take everything here with that in mind, then go check it yourself. Every issuing organization publishes its own requirements. Read them. You will notice something fast. Almost none of them ask the agent to prove they sold anything.

Follow the money

Here is the part almost nobody says out loud. Most of these programs are products. Somebody sells the course. Somebody collects the fee. Many of them collect again every year, because the letters go away if the agent stops paying dues.

That is not a scandal by itself. Schools charge tuition. But it changes how you should read the pitch. When an agent is handed a credential, they are usually also handed a marketing kit, a logo, a badge for their signature, and a script for using it to win listings. The program has an interest in the agent believing it works, because that is what renews.

So when somebody leads with letters, ask the quiet question. Who sold that, what did it cost, and does it have to be repurchased every year to stay true?

This is not a swipe at the people who have them

I spent twenty years in a department full of people with letters after their name. Some of them were the best I ever worked next to. Some of them were bad at the actual job and had a wall full of certificates saying otherwise. The certificate never once told me which was which. The work did.

Same thing here. Plenty of excellent agents in this valley hold these credentials, and some of them earned every hour. A designation is not a red flag. It is just not evidence. Treat it like a resume line, not a guarantee, and then go look at what they actually did.

The license is the harder test

Worth sitting with. To get a California real estate license you take a 150 question state exam, and about half the people who sit for it do not pass. Statewide pass rates have run in the 45 to 53 percent range in recent reporting from the Department of Real Estate.

Now compare that to most of the credentials on this page. A course you attend, a fee you pay, and in many cases no exam you can meaningfully fail. The thing that lets an agent legally sell your house is a harder bar than most of the things they put after their name to prove they are good at it.

What would change my mind

I am not against credentials. I am against treating them as proof. Show me a designation that requires an agent to report their real numbers, list price to sale price, days on market, and closed sides in a defined area, and requires those numbers to stay above a line to keep the letters, and I will sign up for it the same week and pay whatever it costs.

That credential does not exist yet in residential real estate. CRS is the closest, because it makes the agent show production. If one shows up that goes further, this page gets updated and I will tell you I was wrong.

The one thing that is not optional

Forget the letters for a second. In California every agent holds a license, and every license has a public record. You can look up any agent at the Department of Real Estate in about 30 seconds. Name, license number, brokerage, expiration, and any discipline on file.

Do that before you sign anything with anyone, including me. My license is CalDRE #01238257. My broker is Sync Brokerage Inc. Go look.

So why does an agent with no letters know all this?

Because the subjects behind these programs are real, and a seller pays for what an agent does not know. Short sale timelines. Disclosure exposure. Pricing against an appraiser. Tenant rights during showings. A VA appraisal that comes in under contract. I went and learned the material. I skipped the certificate.

That is the whole difference between the two things, and nobody in this business wants to say it out loud. The knowledge and the credential are sold separately.
