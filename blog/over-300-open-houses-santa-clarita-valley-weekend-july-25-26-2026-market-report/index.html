What should you ask the moment you walk into an open house?

Find out who you are actually talking to. This is probably not going to be the favorite section of this article for agents reading it, and I am saying it anyway, because it protects you.

The person greeting you at the door is not always the listing agent. It could be another agent from the same brokerage. It could be an agent from a completely different brokerage who asked to sit the house to meet buyers. It could be an assistant, or somebody who works for the listing agent. All of those are normal and none of them are scandalous. But they matter, because they change how much weight the information you are being handed should carry.

Here is the mechanism that actually hurts people. Somebody at an open house engages you in conversation and tells you things about the residence. You hold onto that. Later you contact your own representative, your own agent, and you convey what you were told. It travels. And if some part of it was not valid, but it was delivered as though it were valid and true, that misunderstanding follows you all the way into an offer and sometimes into escrow. Present company excluded, people sometimes talk a little too much. So find out who they are before you move on.

What does a 694-day listing actually tell you?

It tells you the market gave an answer and nobody accepted it.

Here is what I want sellers to sit with. Every single one of those 255 homes sitting past 60 days had a first week. In that first week the phone rang, people were excited, everybody wanted to go see it. The showings happened. And then the data came back, and the number that came back was more than the seller wanted to absorb and understand as a pricing reality. So the price stayed, and the calendar kept moving.

Some people go out there with big hopes that their property will sell for far more than it is valued at. If you genuinely have a unicorn property, something with no real comparable in the valley, you might pull that card and it might work out for you. But pattern of practice, it does not work out much at all. People shopping for real estate are not doing it in a lackadaisical fashion. They are going out, finding out what these properties are worth, and deciding whether they are worth the salt.

That is what overpricing actually costs. Not a lower sale price. A longer one, and eventually a lower one anyway, from a weaker position, with a days-on-market number attached that every buyer's agent can see.

How do Coming Soon listings work, and how should you use them?

Right now 13 homes are staged to launch in the Coming Soon category, with Saugus leading at 5 listings. Coming Soon has a window of 0 to 21 days, meaning the agent has any amount of time inside that span to move the listing from Coming Soon to active.

This is your preview window, and most buyers waste it. All the Coming Soon listings are posted on the homepage at santaclaritaopenhouses.com, and this is where you get to see what is about to hit before it hits. If you spot something that looks good, the move is to send it to your agent immediately and have them pull a full comparable profile on it. Then you develop your strategy before the property goes active, instead of reacting to somebody else's timeline after it does.

Worth noting: not every new listing goes through Coming Soon. Plenty pop straight onto the market as active without any Coming Soon strategy at all, and that works fine too. Which is why the new inventory section refreshes every few minutes rather than once a day.

How fast are Santa Clarita homes actually selling?

Castaic is moving quick at 14 days on market. Valley-wide, a new home is taking about 48 days to get into escrow. Last week's closings averaged 37 days, and the 30-day figure sits at 27.

Put those next to the 60-day stat and you get the real shape of this market. It is not slow. It is split. Correctly priced homes are moving in two to four weeks. Incorrectly priced homes are joining the 255, and the gap between those two outcomes is almost entirely a pricing decision made in the first week.

Here is what happens when you do hand over your information on one of those sites: you give up your address, your name, and your phone number, and from that moment every agent who bought that zip code has your number. Your phone does not stop ringing. You end up needing what amounts to a spam relocation program. It genuinely chaps my hide that entire businesses get built around harvesting that.

So santaclaritaopenhouses.com is my own system. It has everything, and it is clean. No registration required. None of your personal private information is sold. Just the inventory, the photographs, the open house schedule, the Coming Soon previews, the Mello-Roos information, and the map, updated continuously as the board changes.

What does a genuinely good buyer's agent do differently?

I represent sellers only, which means I am not pitching you here. I was a buyer's agent for over 21 years before I made that choice, and I have buyer's agents I am happy to refer you to. This is just what the good ones do.

A good buyer's agent pulls data on properties outside their client's stated comfort zone. Not to walk it back to the client and say hey, maybe we can squeeze an extra fifty grand out of you. That is the wrong strategy and you should fire anyone who runs it. They do it to find out whether there is genuine wiggle room, or whether a property was simply priced wrong.

If it is priced wrong and the comps prove it, they can go to the listing agent and say: look at this, there is a problem here. My buyer would like to see it. My buyer does not even know it exists, because it sits outside their purchase range. But at this price, it makes sense. Do we have a deal worth exploring? Let me get my client out there.

That is how it is done. Some agents do not have the tenacity to make that call. Others simply are not paying enough attention and leave the heavy lifting in their buyer's hands. You might be perfectly capable of doing it yourself, but there are certain heavy-lifting things you should not have to do, and a good buyer's agent does all of it without stressing you out. That is a real argument for having your own representation, and I am a big advocate for it.

What should you verify in writing before you commit?

The interest rate fluctuates every day, and everything about your financing depends on your credit profile. As the saying goes, marry the house, not the interest rate. But whatever that rate happens to be when you purchase, make sure you know exactly what your all-in payment is going to be. Understand the HOA. Understand the Mello-Roos, which is why I keep a full Mello-Roos section on santaclaritaopenhouses.com so you can see that information yourself.

Then verify it with your lender, and follow it up with them in writing. Same thing with your real estate agent, and I include myself in that. We are not impervious to nonsense. If I give you information as a seller that is going to have real impact on your sale, you should absolutely verify what I told you in writing. And if I came back afterward and said well, maybe you misunderstood me, you should fire me. I would fire me too.
